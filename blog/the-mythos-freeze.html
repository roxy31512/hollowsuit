The Mythos Freeze: Discretionary Enforcement without Institutional Safeguards

June 24, 2026

On 10 June 2026, in an essay on his personal site, Amodei asked for governments to have explicit statutory authority to block model deployments and for that authority to be safeguarded against arbitrary or politically motivated use.

That essay was published two days before the US government ordered Anthropic to shut down access to Claude Fable 5 and Claude Mythos 5 for foreign users.

Since Anthropic could not differentiate foreign nationals from US persons in the short time frame given by the government, the practical response was to shut down both models for everyone.

Regardless of whether or not security concerns were legitimate and substantiated by the US government, the episode showed that without safeguards similar to the ones Amodei wrote about, there was inadequate assessment.

What Amodei Asked For, and What Arrived

In his essay, Amodei argued that governments should have explicit authority to block deployment of an AI model if a third-party assessment finds that it poses unacceptable risk. Although the scope of threats that he set out was narrow (e.g., bioweapons, cyberweapons, loss of control), he attached a condition, noting that "there must be protective measures against political favoritism or arbitrary decisions".

According to Anthropic's public statement, the US government directive explicitly ordered them "to suspend all access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5 by any foreign national, whether inside or outside the United States, including foreign national Anthropic employees".

Some commentators asked if this move by the Trump administration handed OpenAI a competitive edge; a more revealing question is to ask why the US invoked export control law which is built for physical and software transfers to adversaries to force a capability-based product recall.

Export Control Filled a Legislative Gap

Since the US Congress hasn't passed comprehensive AI safety legislation, the Executive branch turned export control law (specifically deemed exports) into a makeshift regulatory hammer.

US export controls are designed to keep physical items and software out of the hands of adversaries, and while the Entity List does this by restricting named parties, deemed export rules target non-US persons. The US government used that to force a blanket, global shutdown of Anthropic's AI models. If finding a non-universal jailbreak that can identify software flaws is the new threshold for a national security recall, then more than just Mythos and Fable should be pulled today (see a cybersecurity lab's report and the UK AI Safety Institute's evaluation).

Export controls are applied to processes that have a transfer with an exporter, a recipient, and a destination; compliance can be checked at each point of transfer.

The US Department of Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security advisory opinions from January 2009 concluded that providing cloud computing services does not constitute exporting the underlying software to the customer (see, Application of EAR to Grid and Cloud Computing Services). An AI model doesn't have these checks built-in in the same way because the product is served continuously, and it isn't clear that chatting with a model counts as releasing controlled technology or source code to a foreign national. Deemed-export rule were built to stop foreign employees inside a company from viewing restricted source code, rather than stop regular AI users from chatting with the model. A Claude user receives outputs in the form of text, code, images, and answers, rather than the blueprint in the form of weights, source code, or the know-how to build the model. So, for the government's position to work, it needs another theory, for example that some outputs, like detailed exploit code, are themselves controlled technology; that the model was classified as a controlled item in a way that makes access regulated; or that it used an authority that doesn't depend on "release" at all.

Also, forbidding all non-US nationals from using the models doesn't map onto the risk defined by capability and misuse. The directive wasn't based on what people did with the model, but who they were. Nationality served as a poor proxy for risk – a US national who could have misused the model would not have been stopped, but allied-country users, researchers, and Anthropic's own staff who posed no identified risk were banned from the models.

Penalizing Transparency

This episode also should show us that unannounced, open-ended shutdown mandated by governments after an AI lab reveals a dangerous capability can penalize transparency and give labs reasons to under-test, under-report, or limit what they share with government. There's also a point about uneven disclosure where the lab that tests a lot and publishes results reveals more than the ones that do so less. But governments can only act on what they see, so enforcement may be following disclosure rather than actual risk, and result in making the most transparent labs look the most dangerous. Regardless of whether this move by the US government was meant to slow down Anthropic in favour of OpenAI, a motive that can't be proven from the outside, unequal treatment of AI labs that are producing frontier models capable of finding and exploiting software vulnerabilities at comparable levels is a process failure (Axios).

The US government might argue that adopting export control in this situation was deemed necessary because deemed-export authority is established law, courts defer on national security, speed may matter for cyber risk, and that Congress didn't leave alternative options. The one I find most convincing is that the government's evidence may be classified, which could explain the lack of a published threshold for applying such bans. My take on this is that classification does limit what is disclosed to the public, but this doesn't justify the absence of a clear process for classified review with a time limit, a stated route to restoration, and advance notice with stated reasons – they all can exist alongside classified reasoning. Even if each government point is granted, none explains why those minimal safeguards were missing.

Governments should certainly act on frontier-model risk – with criteria set in advance by Congress, independent assessment, narrow scope, proportionate remedies, notice, a time limit, and review. Amodei's essay named the risk of arbitrary use and then, two days later, an arbitrary-looking use arrived.
