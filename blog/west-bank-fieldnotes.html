Concrete and Code: Field Notes from the West Bank, 2023

August 2023

In June 2023 I spent two weeks in the West Bank, researching and learning how people, movement, and borders are controlled and how technology is used in the process. The West Bank has become a testing ground for novel border control technologies that scan Palestinians as they move around.

This post is a set of field notes built around five photographs that I took during that trip. I attempt to hold the visible barrier and the invisible one in the same frame, and build connections between what I saw and my conceptual framework of the invisible border wall that I began to develop throughout my doctoral work. The invisible border wall is a set of layered systems of control – (1) externalisation and outsourcing of border control, (2) data collection and sharing, and (3) algorithmic tools that process and help enforce border-work. These three layers build on each other without replacing each other. The technologies, organisations, contracts, and practices that make up the wall create barriers to entry, rights, fairness, and opportunities for migrants and travellers alike.

"the more of your data I gather, the more I understand what it means to be human"

This photograph of a graffiti of Zuckerberg was taken about four meters from Banksy's The Walled Off Hotel in Bethlehem. An Australian artist, Lushsux, painted this large mural portrait and the quote attributed to the graffiti reads: "the more of your data I gather, the more I understand what it means to be human."

The West Bank separation barrier stands at 9 meters and stretches 708 kilometres. The wall’s construction began in 1994 by Israel with the goal of separating it from the West Bank. Since, it has been developed further to include frequent checkpoints and advanced tech solutions.

As I walked along the wall, being completely overshadowed by its menacing presence and intimidated by its armed watchtowers, I thought about the various border tech that was there from automated biometric and facial recognition systems, to fiber-optic vibration sensors embedded in the concrete slabs near the hotel detect physical impact, scaling attempts, or spray-paint tampering. Electronic warfare units and IMSI-catchers operate along the checkpoint corridor to intercept cellular signals, track device locations, and map mobile connections of people moving along the wall.

Zuckerberg's face on the concrete wall collapses the distinction between the maximally visible concrete barrier and the invisible, distributed, ambient technology that brands the concrete wall and the ground with motion sensors, thermal imaging cameras, and unmanned ground radars.

Unlike a physical border wall, the digital barrier that Israel installed along the wall resists being honest about what it is, how it makes decisions, and what decision it has made about border crossers. This makes the invisible wall inescapable even for those who never attempt to cross it.

A soldier checks IDs on a street in H2 Hebron.

In this second image, I was walking through Hebron's H2 zone and the Old City. Hebron is the largest city in the West Bank and the only place in the Occupied Palestinian Territories where Israeli settlements are built inside the urban centre of a major Palestinian city. In 1997 under the terms of the Hebron Agreement between the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) and the Palestine Liberation Organization, Israel retained military and security control over H2 which encompasses the historical Old City.

The Israeli soldier in this image was carrying a mobile device. The device likely had Blue Wolf app on it which Israeli soldiers use in the field to photograph Palestinians on the street. The app matches scans against a central intelligence database (Wolf Pack) and displays a colour-coded status: green (pass), yellow (detain briefly), or red (arrest). The program was gamified among troops, awarding prizes to units that took the most photos of Palestinians to continuously train and build the database.

I was a tourist, and so where these people being asked for IDs. The Palestinians among them were not allowed to move forward.

This was another type of border control, but this border was outsourced, externalised. The border is here, in the middle of a street, materialised in the body of a soldier who was checking papers as he walked through Hebron's H2 zone. Israeli military enforced restrictions on Palestinian movement to protect localised settler enclaves. As I walked through these streets, they were strikingly and unnaturally empty; I later learned that Palestinians are not allowed to drive cars in the streets in H2 and in some areas (like upper Al-Shuhada Street) Palestinian pedestrians are not allowed to walk.

This perpetual border control, within the boundaries of a territory via permanent checkpoints, turnstiles, metal gates, and concrete barriers isolate the Old City from the rest of Hebron. Residents have to go through physical searches, biometric scans, or pass through facial-recognition turnstiles just to run basic errands or go home. It was heartbreaking to see abandoned Ottoman-era stone buildings, crumbling under decades of military closure.

Five cameras, one corner. Old City of East Jerusalem

The third image depicts five cameras mounted on the corner of a building in Old City of East Jerusalem. Visually chaotic and almost brutalist in its honesty about being surveilled, was one of my first impressions of Jerusalem.

Old City is one of the most politically, religiously, and socially sensitive square kilometres on Earth because it has major holy sites for Judaism, Islam, and Christianity. It is also sees frequent protests and political clashes.

These cameras and loudspeakers were likely part of Israeli police’s Mabat 2000 surveillance grid which were installed to surveil the Old City of Jerusalem. These cameras likely cross-reference faces in real-time against state databases and track objects and detect “anomalies” by using behavioural prediction algorithms. Amnesty International and other human rights organisations wrote that the surveillance density in the Old City is so high that there are often one to two cameras every five meters.

Graffiti on the physical barrier separating Ramallah from East Jerusalem at the Qalandiya Checkpoint reads "concrete proof of apartheid"

The fourth image was taken of the physical barrier that separates Ramallah from East Jerusalem at the Qalandiya Checkpoint. The graffiti on the concrete barrier reads “concrete proof of apartheid” and is thinly painted over in blocks of white paint. The pattern indicates a recurring cycle of writing and municipal or military erasure at this specific site.

The Checkpoint was established during the Second Intifada in 2001 as a temporary military roadblock at a road junction. This is one of the main crossing points between Ramallah and East Jerusalem. It’s also one of the busiest and most heavily documented checkpoints in the West Bank. Since 2001, this checkpoint was built into a fortified border terminal complex complete with metal cage walkways, elevated watchtowers, and bulletproof inspection booths.

Here the checkpoint is a contested space as much as a physical barrier. Palestinians and activists use graffiti as a response to power and oppression and a form of symbolic resistance to biometric smart cards that Palestinians must use to cross the checkpoint, face scanners which decide whether to release the gate and let Palestinians through, surveillance cameras that match human faces against military database servers (Wolf Pack), military operators who reside inside bulletproof command rooms and decide whether to halt or redirect movement, and dedicated lanes which automatically open for yellow (Israeli) licence plates, while green (West Bank Palestinian) plates are routed to inspection lanes or blocked from crossing.

The wall, the watchtowers, and the queuing structure are all part of this outsourced, visible performance of control, built to be seen and photographed by outsiders. What the graffiti does is name a condition – apartheid – that is produced across the physical and digital layers of the invisible border wall framework.

Rachel's Tomb Checkpoint, located at the northern entrance of Bethlehem, on the border with Jerusalem.

This last image is of a watchtower and it’s part of the fortified compound around Rachel’s Tomb which sits at the northern entrance to Bethlehem.

This site is disputed holy site among Jews, Christians, and Muslims. After the second Intifada in September 2002, Israel’s security cabinet incorporated the tomb on the Israeli side of the West Bank barrier and surrounded it with a concrete wall and watchtowers. The tomb has long-range Pan-Tilt-Zoom (PTZ) optical and infrared/thermal cameras that monitor the interior compound lanes (used by Israeli visitors/buses) and adjacent Palestinian streets of Bethlehem outside the wall.

These invisible layers of the ‘wall’ – the data, the underground cables, the algorithms – cannot be painted on because they can be barely seen. This makes resistance, and meaningful change, that much more difficult.
